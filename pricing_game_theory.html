Your Pricing Page Is a Game Board

19 October, 2026 · Pricing theory, game theory, and what an AI worker should cost

Suppose we built an AI support agent today. It answers product questions, changes subscriptions, processes refunds inside policy, and sends the genuinely weird cases to a human.

Now we have to price it.

The engineering answer arrives first: count the tokens, add the tool calls, apply a margin, and send an invoice. Very clean. Also probably wrong. The customer does not wake up wanting twelve million tokens. It wants fewer tickets, shorter queues, happier users, and perhaps a smaller support bill.

Okay, charge for outcomes then. Except now we need to decide what an outcome is. If the agent gives a vague answer and the customer silently leaves, did we solve the problem or merely exhaust the person? If one conversation resets a password and another saves a fifty-thousand-dollar renewal, are those really the same unit? If hard tickets are free because they escalate, what stops us from attempting only the easy ones?

A seat price is not much safer. The product becomes more useful as it removes work from human agents, but its revenue falls as the customer needs fewer seats. Wonderful business model, truly. Our success eats our invoice.

This annoyed me enough to go down the pricing-theory rabbit hole. I expected a better formula. I found a game.

A price is not merely a number attached to a product. It is a rule that allocates risk, reveals private information, changes behaviour, and tells every player what to optimize.

That is why pricing theory and game theory keep walking into the same room. The seller knows its costs better than the buyer. The buyer knows its workflow and willingness to pay better than the seller. Competitors watch both of them. Salespeople have their own information and incentives. Once a metric decides the bill, everybody learns how the metric works.

Lemme build the story around one fake company. Call it RelayDesk. None of its numbers are benchmarks; they are just a clean little world in which we can break contracts without upsetting an actual finance team.

The first two numbers are not the price

RelayDesk is talking to a software company with 40,000 support conversations each month. A human agent costs about $35 per loaded hour and spends eight minutes on the average ticket. That is roughly $4.67 of labour per ticket. RelayDesk expects to resolve 60 percent of conversations without a human, so it expects 24,000 resolutions.

Its model and tool cost is $0.12 per attempted conversation. Dividing by the probability of success gives the expected compute cost of one resolution:

\[ \text{compute cost per resolution} = \frac{\$0.12}{0.60} = \$0.20. \]
cost per attempt / probability of resolution

Twenty cents is not the price. It is one boundary. The customer's simplified labour value is another:

\[ 24{,}000\times \$4.67 \approx \$112{,}080 \text{ per month}. \]

That enormous gap looks like free money. It is not. It contains onboarding, monitoring, account support, mistakes, human review, security work, the customer's next-best vendor, and the fact that buyers generally dislike donating their entire surplus to a startup.

A useful first sketch is

\[ C(q)=F+cq, \]

where \(q\) is the amount of service, \(F\) is the account-level fixed cost, and \(c\) is the extra cost of one more unit.Fixed and marginal cost. A fixed cost is incurred even when usage is low, such as integration or security review. Marginal cost is the extra cost caused by one more conversation, token, or outcome. For RelayDesk, \(F\) includes integration, evaluations, support, and monitoring. The $0.12 belongs inside \(c\), but it is not all of \(c\).

The seller would like a price above cost. The buyer accepts a price below the value of choosing RelayDesk instead of its best alternative. That last phrase matters. Manual labour may create $112,080 of apparent value, but if another vendor creates nearly the same result for $35,000, RelayDesk cannot negotiate as though the alternative were a room full of humans.

Scenario: the model provider cuts inference cost in half.

RelayDesk's floor falls, but the customer's support problem has not become half as valuable. A cost-plus price wants to fall immediately. A value-based price does not have to. Still, if the buyer can see the cost collapse and the vendor keeps every dollar, perceived fairness enters the game. Cost and value are separate, but neither side negotiates in a vacuum.

The classic monopoly benchmark relates markup to demand elasticity:

\[ \frac{p-c}{p}=\frac{1}{|\varepsilon|}. \]

The left side is the fraction of price sitting above marginal cost. The right side says that a seller can sustain a larger markup when customers are less sensitive to price.Elasticity, without ceremony. Price elasticity asks how strongly demand changes when price changes. If a 1 percent price increase reduces quantity by about 2 percent, elasticity is roughly \(-2\). The absolute value removes the minus sign. This is the Lerner condition, not a quote calculator.[4] Enterprise founders almost never know \(\varepsilon\) cleanly. A lost deal may be about security, timing, procurement, a missing integration, or price. The formula gives us a direction, not a button.

How a number became a game

The history helped me here. Pricing theory did not progress by finding one increasingly clever way to mark up cost. It progressed by noticing another player, another hidden fact, or another reaction that the previous model had ignored.

Cournot imagined firms choosing quantities. Bertrand's famous response made price itself the strategic move. Hotelling then showed why two sellers need not collapse into one identical commodity: location, taste, convenience, or product capability can soften direct price competition.[1][2][3]

Then the subject moved inside the deal. Nash asked how two parties divide a surplus when either can walk away. Oi asked why Disneyland might charge at the gate and again at the ride. Mussa and Rosen showed how a menu can make customers sort themselves. Holmström asked what happens when effort cannot be seen directly and only a noisy result can be rewarded. Each move changed the object called price. It became a tariff, a menu, a contract, a signal, and eventually a policy that learns.

The latest AI pricing problem is new in costume, not in anatomy. We have a seller with hidden costs, a buyer with hidden value and task difficulty, an outcome that is expensive to verify, and repeated interaction after both sides learn how the rule behaves. A 2026 working paper by Lobel, Sun, and Wu models almost exactly this choice between charging for use and charging for reported success.[10] It is a recent model, not settled consensus, but the old theory has clearly found a new customer.

Four meters, four games

RelayDesk writes four proposals on a whiteboard. They are not four ways to collect the same money. They create four different products.

Meter Illustrative offer Who holds the scary risk? What behaviour does it invite?
Seats $100 per human agent Buyer pays even if the AI is weak Success removes the thing being billed
Tokens or calls Cost plus a markup Buyer owns retries and inefficient prompts Waste can increase vendor revenue
Conversations $0.60 per incoming case Buyer pays for easy and escalated cases Usage becomes predictable, quality stays separate
Resolutions $1.75 per verified result Vendor owns failure and complexity risk Both sides begin litigating “resolved”

A billing metric needs to be close to customer value, measurable, auditable, and hard for either side to manipulate. Nothing satisfies all four perfectly. Each meter is a miniature constitution. It decides who bears uncertainty, what action earns revenue, and which facts must be shared.

Scenario: same volume, wildly different work.

Two customers send 10,000 conversations. One mostly needs password resets. The other sends refund disputes, security incidents, and broken integrations. Conversation pricing treats them as twins. Outcome pricing may attract the harder customer precisely because the vendor promises to absorb failure. This is adverse selection: the contract changes which customers choose it.Adverse selection. One side privately knows its type before contracting. A generous insurance plan attracts higher-risk buyers; a flat outcome price can attract customers with unusually hard tasks.

The metric also changes usage. A fee on every conversation can create a ticking meter in the buyer's head. Teams begin routing only obvious cases to the agent because every experiment has a visible cost. Research on information goods models this psychological cost of pay per use and shows that tariff shape can change both demand and profit.[12] An included allowance or prepaid credits can make the marginal use feel less like a taxi meter, even when the expected bill is similar.

This is why “usage based” is not one design. A raw meter, a bundled allowance, a committed spend, and a platform fee plus overage can charge for the same event while producing different behaviour.

Outcome pricing is a principal-agent problem wearing a nice shirt

The appeal is obvious. The buyer, called the principal in contract theory, wants a result. RelayDesk, the agent, chooses effort and implementation details the buyer cannot fully watch.Principal and agent. The principal delegates work. The agent performs it and usually knows more about the action taken. The problem is to make the agent's private incentives point toward the principal's goal. Pay for the result and their incentives appear aligned.

Appear is doing a lot of work.

RelayDesk proposes the following rule:

\[ \text{billable resolution}=1 \quad\text{only if}\quad \begin{cases} \text{no human takeover within 24 hours},\\ \text{no reopen within 72 hours},\\ \text{policy and safety checks pass},\\ \text{spam and test conversations are excluded.} \end{cases} \]

That looks objective until a customer goes silent. Silence can mean “thanks,” “I gave up,” “I found the answer elsewhere,” or “I am now angrily searching for a competitor.” The vendor benefits from the first interpretation. The buyer benefits from disputing it. The measurement rule has created a new game inside the old one.

Rule Vendor's tempting move Buyer's tempting move
Silence counts as solved Close quickly Dispute silent exits
Escalations are free Cherry-pick easy cases Send every hard case
Every attempt is billed Attempt aggressively Suppress borderline usage

This is not hypothetical. Intercom currently documents an “assumed resolution” when the user leaves after receiving an answer, while later reopens are deducted; it also distinguishes billable procedure handoffs from several non-billable escalations.[18] Sierra argues for agreed outcome definitions and says most escalations are not charged.[19] Decagon supports both conversation and resolution pricing, but says most of its customers choose conversation pricing for predictability and fewer arguments about what was solved.[20]

That disagreement is more useful than a universal best practice. It says observability decides whether an outcome contract works.Observability. An outcome is observable when both sides can measure it reliably enough to contract on it. “API call completed” is easy. “Customer's real problem disappeared” is much harder. HubSpot moved two agents toward completed-task pricing in April 2026. Zendesk says its outcome system uses the acting agent plus a separate evaluation model to verify a resolution.[21][22] The market is not converging on one meter. It is experimenting with different verification systems.

Holmström's moral-hazard result explains the tension. Performance pay becomes more useful when the signal reflects the action we care about, but risk and noisy measurement weaken the contract.[8] Holmström and Milgrom add the nastier multitask warning: reward one measurable task strongly and effort can move away from unmeasured quality.[9]

If RelayDesk is paid only for resolution count, it may improve count while harming tone, safety, coverage, or long-run trust. The fix is not fifty separate prices. It is a primary metric with guardrails: reopen rate, customer satisfaction, policy violations, escalation rate, and minimum coverage. Strong incentives on one noisy number are not alignment. They are a spotlight, and work moves into whatever the spotlight can see.

The menu is a quiet interrogation

The buyer knows things RelayDesk does not. It knows its real ticket complexity, internal labour cost, appetite for budget surprises, and how painful a failed answer would be. Asking “what are you willing to pay?” is unlikely to produce a spiritual commitment to honesty.

So the seller can ask through choices.

Plan Illustrative terms What the choice reveals
Pilot $3,000 for six weeks, capped traffic, jointly scored “I do not yet trust the measured value.”
Predictable $4,000 monthly, 10,000 conversations included, overage after “Budget variance scares me more than vendor failure.”
Outcome $2,500 platform fee plus $1.75 per verified resolution “I trust the audit and want you to carry performance risk.”
Enterprise Annual minimum, resolution bands, SLA, audit clause “I value governance and will trade commitment for rate.”

This is second-degree price discrimination: the seller posts a menu and the buyer's choice reveals its type.First, second, third. First-degree discrimination charges each buyer its exact willingness to pay. Second-degree uses a menu so buyers self-select. Third-degree sets prices for observable groups, such as student or regional pricing. Mussa and Rosen's classic model makes the self-selection logic precise.[7] A recent B2B study by Ghili and Yoon uses won and lost deal data to estimate a nonlinear tariff, meaning a price schedule rather than one flat unit price.[11] Their profit estimate belongs to their educational-services setting, not ours. The transferable lesson is that failed deals contain information too.

Two conditions keep the menu honest:

\[ \underbrace{U_i(\text{chosen plan})\ge U_i(\text{outside option})}_{\text{participation}} \]

\[ \underbrace{U_i(\text{chosen plan})\ge U_i(\text{other plan})}_{\text{incentive compatibility}}. \]

\(U_i\) simply means the value customer type \(i\) receives after price, risk, and effort.Utility. Utility is a compact score for what a player prefers. Here it can be read as business value minus bill, implementation pain, risk, and the value of alternatives. Participation says a plan must beat walking away. Incentive compatibility says the plan intended for a customer must actually be the one that customer prefers.

The second condition is where cute pricing pages go to die. Suppose complex customers know they generate costly cases. A flat outcome rate is especially attractive to them because RelayDesk absorbs the difficulty. Easy customers may choose conversation pricing. The menu has sorted buyers, just in the least profitable direction.

This is why the two-part tariff keeps reappearing:

\[ \text{bill}=F+pq. \]
platform fee + unit rate × measured quantity

The fixed fee pays for the account existing. The variable price follows use or value. Walter Oi made this structure famous through the Disneyland problem: charge at the gate, charge at the rides, or mix both?[6] RelayDesk has the same problem with fewer mouse ears. Integration, security, and monitoring belong at the gate. Conversations or verified outcomes are the rides.

A platform fee is therefore not automatically a random tax added by sales. It can defend against a small but integration-heavy account and against hard customers selecting only a generous variable contract. The mistake is pretending the fee came from compute.

The enterprise price is bargained, not discovered

A self-serve price can look like a vending machine. Enterprise pricing is closer to two people standing over a surplus and arguing about the knife.

The lightest useful version of Nash bargaining is

\[ p^*=\arg\max_p \bigl(U_B(p)-d_B\bigr)^\beta \bigl(U_S(p)-d_S\bigr)^{1-\beta}. \]

This looks more frightening than it is. \(U_B\) and \(U_S\) are the buyer's and seller's payoff from a deal. \(d_B\) and \(d_S\) are what each gets if the deal dies. The parameter \(\beta\) represents bargaining weight.“Arg max.” This means “choose the price that makes the following expression as large as possible.” The formula multiplies each side's gain above walking away. The equation's practical lesson is not that every deal splits fifty-fifty. It is that the outside option enters the price.[5]

Scenario: procurement has two credible finalists.

RelayDesk keeps pointing at $112,080 of labour value. Procurement points at a rival offering nearly the same workflow for $1.40 per resolution. The customer's disagreement payoff just improved. RelayDesk's theoretical value did not vanish, but its differentiated value did.

Now reverse it. The customer has spent six months integrating RelayDesk into permissions, billing, and internal policy. Switching is painful. RelayDesk's renewal leverage rises. It can exploit that once, perhaps. But this is a repeated game. The buyer can fund a replacement project, reduce expansion, refuse a reference, and remember the surprise at the next negotiation.

Game theory makes trust feel less soft. In a one-shot game, extracting every available dollar can be rational. In a repeated relationship, today's concession changes tomorrow's cooperation. Renewal caps, grandfathering, and volume commitments are not just kindness. They can purchase credibility.

The same logic explains discounts. A lower price exchanged for a longer commitment, reference rights, faster payment, or simpler implementation is a trade. A lower price because the salesperson panicked is merely missing data. Record the reason. Negotiation can teach the company, but only if every concession is attached to the fact that caused it.

The competitor is playing too

If two products are identical, easy to compare, and cost the same to deliver, Bertrand competition gives us a brutal instinct: each seller can steal the market with a tiny undercut until price approaches marginal cost.[2] Real B2B software avoids this cliff through differentiation, switching cost, reputation, integrations, and imperfect information.

Hotelling's old spatial picture is useful even when nobody is standing on a beach. Put two vendors on a line. One is cheap and narrow. The other is expensive but can safely execute refunds and account changes. A buyer's effective price includes the distance between what it needs and what the product does. Product position changes how many customers an undercut actually steals.[3]

That is the clean game. Repetition makes it stranger. One firm cuts price, the other undercuts, both fall, then one jumps back up. Maskin and Tirole modeled versions of these Edgeworth cycles in an alternating price game.[14] A public SaaS pricing change can create a slower version: rivals match it, procurement screenshots it, and old customers bring it to renewal. What looked like an experiment has changed everybody's strategy set.

Scenario: the temporary public discount.

RelayDesk cuts its public outcome price for a test. Sales quietly adds extra concessions to save the expensive arm. Competitors match the visible price. Existing customers request the new rate. The experiment did not merely measure demand. It trained the market.

Algorithms make reactions faster. Calvano and coauthors found Q-learning agents sustaining prices above the competitive benchmark in a controlled repeated-oligopoly environment without direct communication.[15] Brown and MacKay show that pricing algorithms with different update frequencies and commitment properties can change equilibrium prices and dispersion.[16]

These are mechanisms, not accusations. A high market price does not prove collusion, and an annual enterprise negotiation is not an online retail bot changing a number every hour. The sober lesson is smaller: changing the speed and rule of response changes the game, even when the underlying products stay the same.

Dynamic pricing is three different things pretending to be one

People use dynamic pricing to describe at least three systems:

  1. A dynamic bill. Quantity changes while the unit rate stays fixed. More API calls create a larger invoice. This is usage billing, not necessarily dynamic pricing.
  2. A rule-based price. The unit rate changes when an observable threshold is crossed, perhaps because volume enters a new band.
  3. A learning price. The seller experiments, estimates demand, and changes the offer to improve an objective.

Only the third is really trynna discover a better price. Its simplest objective is

\[ \pi(p)=(p-c)D(p)-F, \]

where \(\pi\) is profit and \(D(p)\) is demand at price \(p\).Demand function. \(D(p)\) describes how much the market buys at price \(p\). It usually falls as price rises, but enterprise data rarely reveals the curve cleanly. Raising price improves margin on accepted deals and may reduce the number accepted. The seller is learning that curve while earning from it.

This is easy to say and hard to do with five enterprise renewals. Acceptance is sparse, negotiated, and selected. Sales representatives may compensate for a high-price test with more attention or a hidden discount. One deal fails a security review and gets labelled price sensitive. Another accepts because migration has already begun. Randomization on a spreadsheet does not rescue an experiment whose treatment changed in the room.

Cheung, Simchi-Levi, and Wang study demand learning when the seller can change prices only a limited number of times.[13] That is a much better metaphor for enterprise SaaS than a supermarket repricing every minute. RelayDesk should learn at coherent commercial moments: pilots, new offers, expansions, and renewals. It should use broad segments, stable rules, and contribution profit rather than conversion alone.

Personalization adds another game. If two similar buyers compare notes and discover different unexplained prices, demand may change because the procedure feels unfair. Competition makes the welfare effect ambiguous too; theory does not support the lazy claim that more customer data always means more seller profit.[17] A price can be mathematically optimized and commercially radioactive.

Then somebody has to make the contract real

Economics chooses the game. Billing infrastructure makes sure everybody plays the version that was actually signed.

Objectiveprofit, adoption, retention, or learning
Value metricconversation, outcome, workflow, or credit
Eventwhat happened, when, and for which customer
Rate cardfee, bands, overage, discount, or pass-through
Guardrailscommitment, cap, entitlement, alert, and dispute
Invoiceauditable money both sides can reconcile

This is where companies such as Flexprice, Orb, Metronome, and Lago enter. They sit mainly in the execution layers: ingest events, aggregate usage, apply customer-specific rates, manage credits and commitments, preserve legacy plans, and produce billing data.[24][25][26][27]

Flexprice is a particularly neat example because its current product pages connect pricing experiments to the operational mess underneath. It describes testing rate structures on subsets, rolling them back, grandfathering legacy plans, segment overrides, and audit logs.[24] If RelayDesk chooses a $2,500 platform fee plus $1.75 per verified resolution, Flexprice can help turn accepted resolution events into the correct bill.

But this boundary matters: infrastructure can execute a rule without proving the rule is economically optimal. Metering is not demand estimation. A/B assignment is not causal identification if sales changes its behaviour. Calling a changing usage bill dynamic pricing does not make it a learning system. The plumbing can be excellent while the economics is confused.

Forethought's public description of a platform fee plus outcome-based cost is a nice real-world hybrid.[23] Orb and Lago both describe support for tokens, outcomes, credits, commitments, and hybrid plans.[25][27] Their variety tells us something important. The modern pricing product is not one price. It is the ability to encode a contract without rebuilding the product every quarter.

The RelayDesk contract I would actually start with

After all that theory, I would not let an autonomous optimizer loose on five renewals. I would start with a boringly legible hybrid:

\[ \text{monthly bill} =F+p_rR+p_cC+\text{approved tool costs}. \]
platform + standard resolutions + complex outcomes + rare pass-through

Here \(R\) is the number of verified standard resolutions and \(C\) is a separately defined complex outcome class. The contract would begin with a paid six-week proof of value. Both sides would agree on resolution windows, excluded traffic, audit samples, coverage, and quality measures before deployment.

I would offer conversation pricing and verified-outcome pricing rather than declare one morally superior. The buyer can reveal whether it fears budget variance or performance risk more. Annual minimum commitments can purchase lower variable rates. Spend alerts and caps prevent the viral month from becoming a finance incident. No personalized rate changes appear halfway through a contract. Renewal tests happen across coherent groups with explicit grandfathering.

For the large customer in our example, suppose the platform fee is $5,000 and the standard resolution price is $1.75:

\[ \text{bill}=\$5{,}000+\$1.75(24{,}000)=\$47{,}000. \]

Against the simplified $112,080 labour value, the buyer retains about $65,080 before review costs, error costs, implementation work, and the value of alternatives. If RelayDesk also spends $8,000 monthly on account support and its 40,000 attempts cost $0.12 each, simplified account contribution is

\[ \$47{,}000-[40{,}000(\$0.12)+\$8{,}000] =\$34{,}200. \]

These numbers do not prove $1.75 is optimal. They prove the offer is possible and expose the assumptions that could kill it. That is already more useful than multiplying token cost by five and calling the result strategy.

Now try to break it

This is the part of pricing decks that should be much longer than it usually is.

  1. Hard customers select the outcome plan. Resolution probability falls, support rises, and the risk premium was too small.
  2. Verification is weak. Disputes and human audits cost more than the alignment benefit.
  3. A close rival exists. RelayDesk's differentiated value is far below the buyer's total labour saving.
  4. The meter suppresses use. Customers protect their budget by withholding exactly the borderline cases from which the product needs to learn.
  5. The quality scorecard misses the real harm. Resolution rises while trust, safety, or long-run retention quietly falls.
  6. The evidence is too sparse. A pricing model learns the personalities of five procurement teams and mistakes that for a demand curve.

A contract should fail on paper before it fails on an invoice.

What this story does not say

It does not say outcome pricing is always better. It is better only when the outcome is valuable, observable, auditable, and not dangerously incomplete. A conversation can be the more honest meter when success is disputed.

It does not say value-based pricing means charging the customer's entire value. Competition and bargaining determine how much of the surplus the seller can keep. Trust and repeated play determine how much it should try to keep.

It does not say every customer needs a unique price. Personalization can improve fit, but it adds fairness, governance, and explanation costs. A small menu often learns more cleanly than a mysterious model-generated number.

It does not say algorithms collude whenever prices remain high. Laboratory learning agents, dynamic-oligopoly models, online retail evidence, and negotiated B2B software are different settings. The research shows possible mechanisms, not an automatic verdict.

And it does not say tokens have stopped mattering. Tokens affect cost. Seats describe the organization. Conversations measure contact. Outcomes approach value. The error is asking any one of them to explain the whole exchange.

The invoice was the institution

I began with an AI worker and one apparently simple question: what should it cost?

The first answer was twenty cents of compute per resolution. The second was $112,080 of possible labour value. Neither was the price. The price had to survive a buyer with private information, a seller with private costs, a noisy definition of success, a salesperson with discretion, a rival with its own response, and a relationship that continued after the first invoice.

That is what pricing theory added. Cost gives a floor. Value gives a distant ceiling. Elasticity describes how demand pushes back. Differentiation changes how hard competitors can steal. Menus make hidden types reveal themselves. Two-part tariffs divide account costs from usage. Bargaining gives outside options a mathematical seat at the table.

Game theory added the more uncomfortable part. Every rule becomes somebody's strategy. Bill conversations and the buyer may suppress them. Bill outcomes and both sides argue over the event. Reward one quality metric and effort walks away from the others. Publish a discount and the competitor learns too. Optimize every renewal and the loyal customer realizes it has become the experiment.

So the final result is not $1.75, or $0.99, or any other neat number from this essay. It is a method. Measure cost and value separately. Name the outside option. Pick a metric both sides can inspect. Offer a small menu. Put risk with the party best able to control it. Test at moments when a test will not poison the relationship. Then use the billing stack to enforce the promise that economics designed.

The token was never the product. The invoice was never just accounting.

It was the rule of the game.
