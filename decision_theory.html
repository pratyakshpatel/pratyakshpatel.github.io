A Rational Shopper Would Shuffle the Reviews

3 November, 2026 · Decision theory from the ground up: acts, beliefs, utility, models, and the systems that decide alongside us

At some point in my second year of college I was having the very quiet kind of crisis where you keep two browser tabs open for a month. One tab was physics. The other was data science. I liked both, I was not sure which one liked me back, and I was reading far too many papers from both sides in the hope that one of them would make the decision for me.

One of the papers that wandered into the data-science tab was about Amazon reviews. It wasn't about a recommendation engine or a giant neural network. It was about the reviews themselves: the little blocks of text under a product, the stars, and the line that says 312 people found this helpful. It was called Exploring reviews and review sequences on e-commerce platform: A study of helpful reviews on Amazon.in, by Kapil Kaushik, Rajhans Mishra, Nripendra P. Rana, and Yogesh K. Dwivedi, published in the Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services in 2018.[1]

The abstract had the ingredients I expected: star ratings, the volume of reviews, how informative they were, their valence. Then it had one word I kept going back to: sequence. The authors were asking whether the order in which helpful reviews appear affects product sales. They built their model with ideas from message persuasion, cognitive overload, and something called the belief-adjustment model.[1]

That question bothered me in a productive way, because I had just learnt enough probability to have a strong opinion about it. Order should not matter. If ten reviews contain seven happy customers and three unhappy ones, a sensible person should end up believing the same thing whether the complaints come first or last. Multiplication commutes. Bayes' rule does not care which piece of evidence you hand it first. So if the order mattered, then either the shoppers were not being sensible, or my idea of sensible was too small.

Both turned out to be true. Working out why took me through a whole field I had not realised I was walking into. A few months later I was at IIM Nagpur for the summer, interning with Dr. Kaushik on an NLP project. The next summer I went back for a vision-and-language problem, which is how I ended up obsessed with BLIP. Dr. Kaushik works in decision science and information systems.[2] Looking back, his Amazon paper was my first real encounter with that phrase. It was not about models that predict. It was about models that sit next to someone who has to choose.

This essay is the ground-up version of what I wish I had read that semester. We begin with almost nothing, just a table of acts and states. Then we build up through probability, utility, the axioms that justify expected utility, Bayes, the ways real people break those axioms, the value of information, optimal stopping, and finally the systems where a platform's ranking decision and a shopper's buying decision are tangled together. One running example carries the whole thing: a pair of earphones on Amazon.in, ten reviews, and whether their order should change what you buy.

A decision has three moving parts: what you believe, what you want, and what it costs to find out more. Bayes makes the first part ignore order. People break that rule in one direction. A perfectly rational reader who pays for every review breaks it in the opposite direction.

What a decision is made of

Lemme start lower than feels necessary. Here's the situation. You need earphones. There is a budget pair at ₹1,499 with a 4.1-star average and a premium pair at ₹4,999. You could also wait and keep your current pair, the one whose left ear crackles when you turn your head.

Leonard Savage gave us a vocabulary for this that has barely changed since 1954.[3] A state is a complete description of the part of the world you do not control. An act is something you can choose. A consequence is what happens when an act meets a state. Formally, an act is a function from states to consequences:

\[ f : S \to C, \qquad s \mapsto f(s). \]

That definition is less abstract than it looks. "Buy the budget pair" is not a consequence. It is a rule that hands back a different consequence depending on how the world turns out. If the budget pair is fine, you saved ₹3,500. If it is a lemon, you have a crackling right ear now as well as a crackling left ear.Why functions? Treating an act as a function from states to outcomes is what allows us to compare acts state by state. It also makes clear what you control (the function) and what you don't (the argument).

For our earphones I will use three states:

  • \(s_1\): the budget pair turns out fine.
  • \(s_2\): the budget pair is a lemon, and the premium pair would have been fine.
  • \(s_3\): it barely matters, because you lose whatever you buy within a month. Historically, this is my most likely state.

Now fill in how good each consequence feels. For the moment, just pretend these numbers mean something. In a few sections we will earn the right to use them.

Act \(s_1\): budget fine \(s_2\): budget lemon \(s_3\): lost in a month
B: buy budget 8 1 2
P: buy premium 7 6 −1
W: wait 3 3 3

Budget is best if the cheap pair works. Premium is safest against a lemon, but if you lose it, the loss stings more. Waiting is bland in every state. This little matrix is the atom of decision theory. Every later idea either fills in the missing information about which column you are in or questions whether the numbers in the cells deserve to be added up.

One rule needs no extra information at all. An act \(f\) dominates \(g\) if \(f(s)\) is at least as good as \(g(s)\) in every state and strictly better in at least one. Nobody argues with dominance. If some act is worse no matter what happens, throw it away. The trouble is that dominance almost never settles anything interesting. In our table, no act dominates another. Each one wins somewhere.

Choosing when you refuse to guess

Suppose you refuse to say how likely each state is. That sounds like a reasonable position. It's a new product, the reviews could be fake, and who are you to put a probability on a lemon? Decision theorists spent the early 1950s writing down rules for exactly this mood, which they called decisions under ignorance.[4][5][6]

Maximin is the pessimist's rule. For each act, look at its worst outcome and choose the act whose worst is least bad. Abraham Wald built much of statistical decision theory around this minimax idea.[4] The worst cases are \(1\), \(-1\), and \(3\), so maximin says wait.

Maximax is the optimist's rule. Look at each act's best outcome. The best cases are \(8\), \(7\), and \(3\), so maximax says buy budget.

Leonid Hurwicz proposed a dial between the two. Pick an optimism level \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) and score each act as

\[ H_\alpha(f)=\alpha\max_s f(s)+(1-\alpha)\min_s f(s). \]

For our table, \(H_\alpha(B)=1+7\alpha\), \(H_\alpha(P)=-1+8\alpha\), and \(H_\alpha(W)=3\). Budget overtakes waiting once \(1+7\alpha \gt 3\), or \(\alpha \gt 2/7\). Premium would need \(-1+8\alpha \gt 1+7\alpha\), or \(\alpha\gt 2\), which is impossible. Under Hurwicz, the premium pair never wins for any optimism level.

Savage suggested a different emotion to minimise: regret.[5] For each state, regret is how much worse you did than the best act available in that state. You never look across states, only down each column:

\[ r(f,s)=\max_{g} g(s)-f(s), \qquad \text{choose } \arg\min_f \max_s r(f,s). \]

The same nine numbers give three different answers. That is not a failure of the rules. Each one encodes a different attitude, and in 1954 John Milnor showed that no rule for decisions under ignorance can satisfy every reasonable-sounding condition at once.[7] Maximin ignores everything except one cell per row. Regret makes the choice depend on which other acts happen to be on the menu: add a silly fourth option and the regret ranking of the first three can flip.Menu dependence. Minimax regret violates "independence of irrelevant alternatives." A new act can raise the best value in some column, which changes every other act's regret. Then your choice between B and P depends on whether a third product is listed.

Notice what Laplace quietly did. It assigned probabilities, \(1/3\) each. The moment you admit that some states are more plausible than others, you are no longer deciding under ignorance. And this is the whole point of reading reviews: reviews are evidence about which column you are in.

Let's put numbers on that. Write \(\mathbb E_p[f]=\sum_s p(s)f(s)\) for an act's expected score under beliefs \(p\). If you believe \(p=(0.6,0.3,0.1)\), the expected scores are \(5.3\), \(5.9\), and \(3\), and you buy premium. If the reviews push you to \(p=(0.8,0.1,0.1)\), the scores become \(6.7\), \(6.1\), and \(3\), and you buy budget. Waiting falls away as soon as you hold any reasonable belief. It only won under maximin, which considers nothing but the worst case.

Keep \(p(s_3)=0.1\) fixed, since losing earphones is a fact about me rather than the product, and let \(q\) be your probability that the budget pair is good. Then \(p(s_1)=0.9q\), \(p(s_2)=0.9(1-q)\), and

\[ \mathbb E[B]=1.1+6.3q,\qquad \mathbb E[P]=5.3+0.9q,\qquad B\succ P \iff q\gt \tfrac{7}{9}\approx 0.778 . \]
the whole purchase reduces to one belief crossing one line

Hold on to that \(7/9\). For the rest of the essay, the review page has a single job: to move \(q\) across that line or leave it where it is.

How a gambling letter became a theory of choice

I used to think of decision theory as one tidy subject: maximise expected utility, done. The history is messier and much more fun. It has three threads that keep tugging at each other. One thread asks what a rational chooser should do. A second asks what people actually do, and keeps finding that it is something else. A third, younger thread asks what happens when the chooser is a machine that has to decide again and again while it learns.

The top lane begins with a gambling problem. In 1654, Pascal and Fermat exchanged letters about how to split the stakes of a game of chance that had been interrupted halfway. Their answer was to weight each possible ending by its probability, and that idea became expected value.[8] Bernoulli then showed that expected money could not be the whole story. Bayes's essay, published after his death, showed how evidence should change belief. Ramsey argued that a belief is a betting rate. Then von Neumann and Morgenstern, almost in passing while founding game theory, proved that four plain axioms about preferences force you to maximise an expected utility.[13] Savage finished the structure by deriving both the probabilities and the utilities from choices alone.[3]

The middle lane is the theory's shadow. It barely waited for the axioms to dry. In 1953, Maurice Allais handed economists a pair of bets on which most of them broke the independence axiom.[15] Herbert Simon argued that real minds do not maximise at all; they search until something is good enough.[17] Ellsberg showed that people treat unknown probabilities differently from known ones.[16] Tversky and Kahneman spent the 1970s cataloguing the shortcuts behind judgment, then proposed prospect theory as a descriptive replacement.[18][19] Hogarth and Einhorn's 1992 model of order effects in belief updating is the piece that leads straight to the Amazon paper.[21][1]

The bottom lane starts, oddly, earlier than von Neumann. In 1933 William Thompson asked how to allocate patients between two treatments when you are still learning which one works. His answer, sample from your current belief and act as if the sample were true, sat mostly unused for decades before it started running large chunks of the internet.[28][32] Wald recast statistics itself as a decision problem. Bellman turned repeated decisions into a recursion. Howard put a price on information. Gittins and then Lai and Robbins worked out what it costs to learn while you earn.[4][27][24][29][30] In this essay we'll walk the top lane first, then the middle, then the bottom, and at the end the three lanes meet on one review page.

Probability as a price you would pay

Before we can weight utilities by probabilities, we have to say what a probability is when it describes one pair of earphones. It can't really be a long-run frequency. You are not buying this exact pair a thousand times. Ramsey in 1926 and de Finetti in 1937 gave the answer that decision theory runs on: your probability for an event is the price at which you would be equally happy to buy or sell a ticket that pays ₹1 if the event happens.[11][12]

That sounds like a loose definition, but it has teeth. Suppose you announce that the budget pair is good with probability 0.6, and separately, because the one-star reviews scared you, that it is a lemon with probability 0.6. A bookie sells you a ₹1 ticket on "good" for ₹0.60 and a ₹1 ticket on "lemon" for ₹0.60. Exactly one of them will pay. You have spent ₹1.20 to receive ₹1.00, and you lose 20 paise in every state of the world. This is called a Dutch book.Dutch book, without ceremony. A set of bets you would accept one by one, but which together lose money no matter what happens. Avoiding sure loss is a much weaker demand than being right. It only asks you not to contradict yourself.

Coherence (Ramsey, de Finetti).

For bets on a finite set of mutually exclusive, exhaustive events \(E_1,\dots,E_n\), your betting prices \(P(E_i)\) are immune to every Dutch book if and only if

\[ P(E_i)\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n P(E_i)=1,\qquad P(E_i\cup E_j)=P(E_i)+P(E_j)\ \text{for } i\ne j. \]

This was the first real surprise of the subject for me. The axioms of probability are not just conventions mathematicians agreed on. They are what consistent betting prices have to look like. A shopper whose beliefs about a product violate them can be made to lose money for sure. Being "Bayesian" therefore starts as a claim about consistency, not about being smart.

Why expected money is the wrong thing to maximise

Expected value was the original definition of a fair price. Nicolaus Bernoulli broke it with a game that his cousin Daniel later analysed in a paper for the St Petersburg Academy, which is how the game got its name.[9]

Toss a fair coin until it lands heads. If the first heads arrives on toss \(k\), you win ₹\(2^k\). How much would you pay to play? The expected payout is

\[ \mathbb E[X]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^k}\cdot 2^k=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1=\infty. \]

No sane person pays even ₹50, let alone everything they own. Daniel Bernoulli's answer is one of the most important sentences in economics: the value of an item should be based not on its price but on the utility it yields.[9] A rupee means more when you have few of them. He proposed logarithmic utility, \(u(x)=\ln x\), and with it the game becomes worth a finite amount:

\[ \mathbb E[\ln X]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k\ln 2}{2^k}=\ln 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k}{2^k}=2\ln 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{certainty equivalent}=e^{2\ln 2}=₹4. \]

The step \(\sum_k k/2^k=2\) is the mean of a geometric distribution, which is a nice thing to verify on a napkin.Bernoulli's version includes wealth. He actually evaluated \(\ln(w+\text{payout}-\text{price})\) for a person with wealth \(w\). The fair price rises with wealth, but only slowly. Later writers built super-St-Petersburg games with payouts growing fast enough to break log utility too, which is one reason many theorists insist that utility be bounded. A certainty equivalent is the sure amount you would accept in place of the gamble. Under log utility, an infinite expectation shrinks to ₹4.

The lesson for our earphones is a quiet one. The numbers 8, 1, and 2 in the table were never meant to be rupees. They have to be something whose average is meaningful, and Bernoulli did not tell us what licenses that averaging. That took two more centuries.

Four axioms and a ruler

Von Neumann and Morgenstern asked the question in reverse. Forget utility for a moment. Suppose you only have preferences over lotteries, meaning probability distributions over outcomes. What conditions on those preferences would force them to look like expected utility?[13]

Write a lottery as \(L=(p_1,x_1;\dots;p_n,x_n)\), and write the mixture \(\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N\) for the compound lottery that runs \(L\) with probability \(\alpha\) and \(N\) otherwise. The four axioms are:

  1. Completeness. For any \(L, M\): \(L\succeq M\) or \(M\succeq L\). You can always compare.
  2. Transitivity. If \(L\succeq M\) and \(M\succeq N\), then \(L\succeq N\). Otherwise someone can cycle you through trades and charge a fee each time.
  3. Continuity. If \(L\succ M\succ N\), there is some \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) with \(\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N\sim M\). No outcome is infinitely good or infinitely bad.
  4. Independence. \(L\succeq M\) if and only if \(\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N\succeq \alpha M+(1-\alpha)N\) for every \(N\) and every \(\alpha\in(0,1]\). Mixing both options with the same third lottery should not flip your preference.
The expected utility theorem (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944; axioms in the 1947 edition).

A preference \(\succeq\) over lotteries on a finite set of outcomes satisfies the four axioms if and only if there is a function \(u\) with

\[ L\succeq M \iff \sum_i p_i\,u(x_i)\ \ge\ \sum_i q_i\,u(x_i). \]

Moreover, \(u\) is unique up to a positive affine transformation: \(v\) represents the same preferences if and only if \(v=a u+b\) with \(a\gt 0\).

The proof is short enough that I want to show the idea. Let \(\bar x\) be the best outcome and \(\underline x\) the worst. For any outcome \(x\) in between, continuity guarantees a probability \(u(x)\) that makes you indifferent:

\[ x\ \sim\ u(x)\,\bar x+\bigl(1-u(x)\bigr)\,\underline x. \]

That number is the definition of utility. Now take any lottery \(L\). Independence lets you replace each outcome \(x_i\) inside \(L\) with its equivalent best-or-worst gamble without changing how much you like \(L\). After all the replacements, \(L\) has turned into a lottery over only two outcomes, \(\bar x\) and \(\underline x\), and the chance of getting the best one is \(\sum_i p_i u(x_i)\). Between two best-or-worst lotteries, the one with the higher chance of the best outcome wins. So you prefer whichever lottery has higher expected utility.Where each axiom works. Completeness and transitivity give a ranking. Continuity creates the number \(u(x)\). Independence lets you substitute it inside a bigger lottery. The independence axiom does most of the heavy lifting, and it is the one people break most often.

This gives the 8 in our table a real meaning. On a scale where the worst earphone outcome is 0 and the best is 10, "budget pair, works fine" scores 8 because you would be indifferent between getting it for sure and taking an 80 percent chance at the best outcome with a 20 percent chance at the worst. Utility is a ruler, and you choose where its zero and its unit go, the way Celsius and Fahrenheit place 0 and 100 at different temperatures. Ratios of utility differences survive the choice of ruler. Statements like "twice as happy" do not.

Once utility exists, its shape describes your attitude to risk. If \(u\) is concave, Jensen's inequality gives \(\mathbb E[u(X)]\le u(\mathbb E[X])\). A gamble is then worth less to you than its average for certain, and you are risk averse.

John Pratt and Kenneth Arrow turned the curvature into a number. The coefficient of absolute risk aversion is

\[ A(x)=-\frac{u''(x)}{u'(x)},\qquad \pi\approx\tfrac12 A(\bar x)\,\sigma^2, \]

where \(\pi\) is the risk premium, \(\bar x\) is the gamble's mean, and \(\sigma^2\) its variance.[14] For \(u=\sqrt x\), \(A(x)=1/(2x)\). For the gamble in the figure, \(\sigma^2=2.5\times10^7\), so the approximation gives \(\pi\approx\tfrac12\cdot 10^{-4}\cdot 2.5\times 10^{7}=₹1{,}250\). The true premium is ₹2,500. The approximation is off by a factor of two, and that is exactly why Pratt's paper is called Risk Aversion in the Small and in the Large. The formula is a second-order Taylor expansion, so it only holds for small gambles relative to wealth.

Savage then took the final step.[3] Von Neumann and Morgenstern took probabilities as given, like the odds on a roulette wheel. Savage started with nothing but preferences over acts, functions from states to consequences like the ones in our first table. From seven postulates he derived both a probability \(P\) over states and a utility \(u\) over consequences:

\[ f\succeq g\iff \sum_{s\in S}P(s)\,u\bigl(f(s)\bigr)\ \ge\ \sum_{s\in S}P(s)\,u\bigl(g(s)\bigr). \]

The postulate everyone remembers is the sure-thing principle. If you would prefer \(f\) to \(g\) when you know some event \(E\) happens, and you would also prefer \(f\) to \(g\) when you know \(E\) does not happen, then you should prefer \(f\) to \(g\) when you do not know. It sounds unarguable, and Ellsberg will argue with it shortly. For now it completes the justification for what we did with the earphones. The table supplies \(u\), your beliefs supply \(P\), and a coherent shopper picks the act with the highest weighted sum.

The reviews as evidence

Now we can finally read the review page as a decision theorist. Simplify the world to two hypotheses about the budget pair: it is Good or a Lemon. Each review is either positive (four or five stars) or negative (one or two). Good products still get some angry reviews, and lemons still get some happy ones, so a review is a noisy signal:

Likelihoodpositive reviewnegative review
if G (good)0.80.2
if L (lemon)0.40.6

Bayes' rule is easiest to see in odds form.[10] Posterior odds equal prior odds times the likelihood ratio of the evidence:

\[ \underbrace{\frac{P(G\mid r)}{P(L\mid r)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} =\underbrace{\frac{P(G)}{P(L)}}_{\text{prior odds}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(r\mid G)}{P(r\mid L)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}}. \]

A positive review has likelihood ratio \(0.8/0.4=2\): it doubles the odds. A negative review has ratio \(0.2/0.6=1/3\): it cuts the odds to a third. Take logarithms and multiplication becomes addition. Each positive review adds \(\ln 2\) to your log-odds, and each negative review subtracts \(\ln 3\). If reviews are independent given the product's quality, then after \(k\) positive and \(m\) negative reviews,

\[ \log\frac{q_n}{1-q_n}=\log\frac{q_0}{1-q_0}+k\ln 2-m\ln 3 . \]
a sum, so the order of the terms cannot matter

Start at \(q_0=0.5\) and read seven positive and three negative reviews. The posterior odds are \(2^7/3^3=128/27\approx 4.74\), so \(q=128/155\approx 0.826\). That is above \(7/9\approx0.778\): buy the budget pair. Now try two orders. With the bad news first, your belief goes \(0.5\to0.25\to0.10\to0.036\), then climbs back through \(0.07, 0.13, 0.23, 0.37, 0.54, 0.70\) and lands at \(0.826\). With the bad news last, it climbs \(0.5\to0.67\to0.80\to\dots\to0.992\), then falls \(0.977\to0.934\to0.826\). The paths look completely different, but they end at the same point.

Order invariance.

If the reviews \(r_1,\dots,r_n\) are conditionally independent given the hypothesis, then for every permutation \(\sigma\) of \(\{1,\dots,n\}\),

\[ P(G\mid r_1,\dots,r_n)=P(G\mid r_{\sigma(1)},\dots,r_{\sigma(n)}). \]

The posterior depends on the data only through the counts \((k,m)\). Those counts are a sufficient statistic.

This is the sense of the title. A Bayesian could shuffle the reviews before reading them and lose nothing. De Finetti showed the idea runs deeper than our two-hypothesis toy.[12] Call a sequence of binary reviews exchangeable if every reordering has the same probability. De Finetti's representation theorem says an infinite exchangeable sequence behaves as if there were an unknown satisfaction rate \(\theta\), with reviews independent given \(\theta\):

\[ P(r_1,\dots,r_n)=\int_0^1\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}\,d\mu(\theta). \]

If \(\mu\) is a Beta\((a,b)\) prior, reading \(k\) positive reviews out of \(n\) gives the posterior Beta\((a+k,\,b+n-k)\), with mean \((a+k)/(a+b+n)\). This is the conjugate-prior pattern that every Bayesian course teaches in week three. Keep it in your pocket, because the platform will use the exact same object when we get to ranking.

Order of updating versus position as evidence.

Real review pages are not exchangeable. Amazon sorts by helpfulness, so the top review is not a random draw. Early reviewers may be enthusiasts, and later ones may be reacting to the early ones. A Bayesian can handle this, but the information goes into the likelihood: "a review in position one is more likely to be careful." Bayes still never cares about the order in which the facts are processed. What it can care about is where a review was placed, if the placement itself tells you something.

How people actually update

Here is where the Amazon paper's theory section sent me. Robin Hogarth and Hillel Einhorn's 1992 belief-adjustment model was built to explain a pile of experiments where the order of evidence did change people's final judgments.[21] In its step-by-step form, you hold a current belief \(S_{k-1}\in[0,1]\), read a piece of evidence, score it as \(s(x_k)\in[-1,1]\), and adjust:

\[ S_k=S_{k-1}+w_k\,s(x_k), \qquad w_k=\begin{cases} \alpha\,S_{k-1} & \text{if } s(x_k)\le 0,\\[2pt] \beta\,(1-S_{k-1}) & \text{if } s(x_k)\gt 0. \end{cases} \]

Read the weights slowly, because they hold the whole psychology. Negative evidence moves you in proportion to how much belief you have left to lose. Positive evidence moves you in proportion to how much room you have to rise. A glowing review is a bigger surprise to a sceptic. A harsh review hits harder when you were nearly convinced. Hogarth and Einhorn call this a contrast effect.Two processing modes. Hogarth and Einhorn distinguish step-by-step updating, where you adjust after every item, from end-of-sequence updating, where you take everything in and adjust once. Their model predicts recency for short, simple, mixed sequences processed step by step, and can predict primacy for longer or more complex ones.

It looks almost Bayesian, but it does not commute. Take \(\alpha=\beta=1\) and evidence of fixed strength \(s\). One positive followed by one negative leaves you at \((S+(1-S)s)(1-s)\). One negative followed by one positive leaves you at \(S(1-s)+(1-S(1-s))s\). Subtract the two and almost everything cancels:

\[ S_{\,-\,\text{then}\,+}\;-\;S_{\,+\,\text{then}\,-}\;=\;s^2 . \]
the commutator of two reviews: whichever came last wins by s²

The gap does not even depend on where you started. Whatever came last pulls you a little further. That is recency, written as a single line of algebra. Now run the same ten reviews through this model with \(s=0.2\). With the bad news first, you end at \(0.844\). With the bad news last, you end at \(0.458\). The Bayesian ends at \(0.826\) both times.

That last line is the real point. It is not just that the two readers' numbers differ. They act differently. Seeing the bad news last, the belief adjuster ends up below \(7/9\) and buys premium, while the Bayesian buys budget. Sales are made of acts, not beliefs, and this is how an order effect in someone's head can show up in an order effect on a sales chart.

The Amazon paper asked whether sequence matters for sales on a real platform, using this theory alongside cognitive overload and message persuasion.[1] For the size and direction of the effects they estimated, read the paper itself. It is the empirical half of this story, and I'm only giving you the theory half. What hooked me was simply that the question could be asked. Tversky and Kahneman's anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic, where people start from an initial value and adjust insufficiently, is the older cousin of the same idea.[18]

Where the axioms crack

Order effects break Bayes. Other experiments break expected utility itself. The most famous is Allais's, in rupees here.[15]

1A₹1 million for certainA

2C11%: ₹1M, 89%: nothing
D10%: ₹5M, 90%: nothingD

Both choices sound reasonable. Who risks a sure million for a 1 percent chance of nothing? But if one option already has a 90 percent chance of nothing, why not reach for the bigger prize? Now write the choices in expected utility with \(u(0)=0\):

\[ A\succ B\iff 0.11\,u(1)\gt 0.10\,u(5), \qquad D\succ C\iff 0.10\,u(5)\gt 0.11\,u(1). \]

These are the same inequality pointing in opposite directions. No utility function can produce both choices. The culprit is independence. Both pairs share an 89 percent slice. In the first pair it is ₹1M, and in the second it is nothing. Independence says a common slice should not matter, and people's choices say it does: certainty has a pull of its own.

Ellsberg went after Savage's sure-thing principle.[16] An urn holds 30 red balls and 60 that are black or yellow in an unknown mix. Most people would rather bet on red than on black. Most people would also rather bet on "black or yellow" than "red or yellow." The first choice implies \(P(R)\gt P(B)\). The second, after cancelling the shared yellow, implies \(P(B)\gt P(R)\). People are not misjudging a probability here. They dislike not knowing the probability, and that dislike is called ambiguity aversion.

Scenario: the flawless product with three reviews.

Product X has three reviews, all positive. Product Y has 2,400 positive out of 3,000. With a uniform prior, X's posterior is Beta(4, 1) and Y's is Beta(2401, 601). Both have mean 0.80. But X's standard deviation is about 0.16, while Y's is about 0.007. An expected-utility shopper whose utility is linear in \(\theta\) is exactly indifferent. Almost every real shopper picks Y. That is Ellsberg, live on a product page. Review volume, one of the variables in the Amazon paper, matters partly because it turns ambiguity into risk.

Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory was built to describe these patterns rather than condemn them.[19][20] It changes two things. Outcomes are judged as gains and losses relative to a reference point, through a value function that is concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper for losses. Probabilities are passed through a weighting function that inflates small chances and deflates large ones. Using the median parameters from their 1992 paper (the weighting parameter shown is the one for gains),

\[ v(x)=\begin{cases}x^{0.88}&x\ge 0\\-2.25\,(-x)^{0.88}&x\lt 0\end{cases}, \qquad w(p)=\frac{p^{\gamma}}{\bigl(p^{\gamma}+(1-p)^{\gamma}\bigr)^{1/\gamma}},\ \ \gamma=0.61. \]

A one-star review usually describes a loss: "died after two weeks," "the left ear stopped working," "refund took a month." A five-star review usually describes a gain against a reference point the shopper already expected to reach. Under loss aversion, the asymmetry is predictable. Chevalier and Mayzlin found in book sales on Amazon.com and Barnes & Noble that a one-star review had a larger effect than a five-star one.[23] Mudambi and Schuff found that what readers rate as helpful depends on review depth, on how extreme the rating is, and on whether the product is one you can judge from specifications or only by using it.[22] Valence, the third ingredient in the Amazon paper, is a loss-gain variable in disguise.[1]

None of this makes expected utility useless. It means there are two theories with two jobs. The normative theory says what a coherent decision looks like. The descriptive theory says what people actually do. A platform that wants to understand its sales needs the second. A person who wants to avoid getting Dutch-booked needs the first. The interesting engineering, as we'll see, happens when one party uses the descriptive theory to design the environment in which the other party makes decisions.

What review number eleven is worth

So far the shopper has been handed a fixed pile of reviews. Real shoppers choose how many to read, and that choice is a decision too. Ronald Howard turned this into a formula in a four-page paper in 1966.[24] The question is: how much should you pay to learn something before you act?

Start with the extreme case, a perfect oracle that tells you whether the budget pair is good. With the oracle, you would pick the best act in each state and then average over your current belief. Without it, you pick one act now. The difference is the expected value of perfect information:

\[ \mathrm{EVPI}(q)=\mathbb E_{\theta}\Bigl[\max_a U(a,\theta)\Bigr]-\max_a \mathbb E_{\theta}\bigl[U(a,\theta)\bigr]. \]

In our setup, if the oracle says G, buy budget and get \(7.4\). If it says L, buy premium and get \(5.3\). So \(\mathrm{EVPI}(q)=7.4q+5.3(1-q)-\max(1.1+6.3q,\ 5.3+0.9q)\). At \(q=0.7\) that is \(6.77-5.93=0.84\). It is zero at \(q=0\) and \(q=1\), where you already know what to do, and it peaks at \(0.93\) exactly on the threshold \(q=7/9\).

That shape gives the most useful sentence in this section. Information is valuable only to the extent that it could change what you do. If no possible answer would flip your choice, the answer is worth exactly zero to you, however interesting it is.A practical test. Before ordering an analysis, a survey, or another dashboard, ask: "Which result would make us choose differently?" If nobody can name one, the analysis has no decision value, whatever else it is good for.

A review is not an oracle. It is a noisy signal, and its worth is the expected value of sample information. You average, over the possible reviews, the value of acting optimally after seeing each one, then subtract the value of acting now:

\[ \mathrm{EVSI}(q)=\sum_{r\in\{+,-\}}P(r\mid q)\,\max_a\mathbb E\bigl[U(a)\mid q_r\bigr]\;-\;\max_a\mathbb E\bigl[U(a)\mid q\bigr]. \]

At \(q=0.7\), a positive review arrives with probability \(0.8(0.7)+0.4(0.3)=0.68\) and lifts you to \(0.824\), which is across the line, so you would switch to budget. A negative review drops you to \(0.44\), and you stay with premium. Laid out as a decision tree:

This "solve from the right" procedure is backward induction. If you have read my trees essay, note the name clash. Those decision trees learn splits from data. These decision trees evaluate choices against uncertainty. Same drawing, almost opposite jobs.

Now for the strange part. At \(q=0.5\), EVSI is exactly zero. One positive review moves you to \(0.667\), which is still below \(7/9\), so a single review can never change your act. A myopic shopper at \(q=0.5\) would never read anything. Yet two positive reviews would move you to \(0.80\), across the line. One review is worth nothing, but the option to read several is worth something. To capture that, we need to think about the whole sequence of reading decisions at once.

The rational reader stops early

Herbert Simon's objection to classical rationality was never that people are stupid. It was that search is costly. A mind with limited attention should search until something is good enough, then stop.[17] The Amazon paper's cognitive-overload theory says the same thing in a different vocabulary: past some point, more reviews add burden rather than clarity.[1] We can make that precise without giving up rationality. We just charge a cost \(c\) for each review read.

Let \(V(q)=\max(1.1+6.3q,\ 5.3+0.9q)\) be what you get if you stop and buy now. Let \(V^\star(q)\) be what you get if you play optimally from belief \(q\), with the option to keep reading. Richard Bellman's principle of optimality says the optimal plan must also be optimal from wherever it takes you next.[27] That gives a fixed-point equation:

\[ V^\star(q)=\max\Bigl\{\,V(q),\ \ -c+\sum_{r\in\{+,-\}}P(r\mid q)\,V^\star(q_r)\Bigr\}. \]
stop and buy now, or pay c, read one, and play optimally from there

I solved this numerically by value iteration on a fine grid of beliefs. With \(c=0.05\), the solution has a clean shape. There is an interval of beliefs, \(0.279\lt q\lt 0.947\), where reading on is worth more than stopping. Outside it, you stop and buy. Cheaper reading widens the interval. At \(c=0.02\) it is \((0.094,\ 0.979)\), and at \(c=0.1\) it shrinks to \((0.524,\ 0.900)\). This is the same structure as Wald's sequential probability ratio test, which keeps sampling while the evidence sits between two boundaries and stops when it crosses one.[26]

Now run the two orders again, with a rational reader who pays \(c=0.05\) per review. With the bad news first, the first review is negative, \(q\) drops to \(0.25\), that is outside the band, and the reader stops after one review and buys premium. With the bad news last, the reader sees five positive reviews, \(q\) rises \(0.67, 0.80, 0.89, 0.94, 0.97\), leaves the band at the top, and buys budget. The three negative reviews at the end are never read.

This table is the thing I would have loved to see in second year. Order effects are not automatic evidence of irrationality. A perfectly coherent Bayesian who pays for attention shows a primacy effect, because the first reviews decide whether it reads any more. A human-like belief adjuster who reads everything shows recency, because the last reviews push hardest. The same data can produce opposite order effects through different mechanisms. That is why the empirical question in the Amazon paper is a real question and not something theory can answer from an armchair.

It also shifts the spotlight. On a page where most shoppers stop early, the first few reviews are not a sample of the evidence. They are most of the evidence that will ever be read. Whoever decides which reviews come first is making a decision with large consequences, and that decider is not the shopper.

A model is not a decision

Here is a confession from the data-science tab. For a long time, when I trained a classifier, the decision step was an afterthought: take the predicted probability and threshold it at 0.5. Decision theory says that threshold is a utility function in disguise.

Wald saw this in 1950 when he recast statistics itself as decision-making.[4] A decision rule \(\delta\) maps data to actions. A loss \(L(\theta,a)\) says how bad each action is in each state. The rule's risk is \(R(\theta,\delta)=\mathbb E_\theta\bigl[L(\theta,\delta(X))\bigr]\). A Bayesian rule minimises expected loss under the posterior. Estimation, testing, and classification all become special cases of choosing an act.

Take the platform's fake-review detector. It outputs \(p=P(\text{fake}\mid\text{text})\). Removing a genuine review costs \(c_{\mathrm{FP}}\): an honest customer is silenced and the evidence pool loses a real data point. Keeping a fake costs \(c_{\mathrm{FN}}\): the evidence is poisoned for every later reader. Remove the review exactly when the expected loss of keeping it is larger:

\[ p\,c_{\mathrm{FN}}\gt (1-p)\,c_{\mathrm{FP}} \iff p\gt \frac{c_{\mathrm{FP}}}{c_{\mathrm{FP}}+c_{\mathrm{FN}}}. \]
the threshold comes from the losses, not from the model

If a kept fake is four times as harmful as a wrongly removed genuine review, the threshold is \(1/5=0.2\), not \(0.5\). The 0.5 default only says that both mistakes cost the same, and almost nothing in a real system satisfies that.[39] This is also why calibration matters more than people usually say. A decision consumes probabilities. If the model's "0.3" does not mean 30 percent, the threshold is applied to the wrong number.

So the vocabulary lines up neatly. A model is a belief: it supplies \(P\). A loss function is a utility: it supplies \(u\). A decision rule combines them. A system is everything that happens next: who sees the output, what they do, and how their actions change the data the model will be retrained on. Simon described managerial decision-making in three phases: intelligence (noticing that a decision is needed), design (constructing the options), and choice.[37] Gorry and Scott Morton used that framing in 1971 to define decision support systems, meaning software that helps a person through the poorly structured parts of a choice.[38] That is the field Dr. Kaushik's title refers to. I now read "decision science and information systems" as one idea, not two.

When the platform is also deciding

Go back to the review page and look at it from the other side. The platform has to choose an order. Its options are rankings. Its states include how useful each review really is. Its consequences include sales, returns, trust, and whether people bother to write reviews at all. Its information comes from helpfulness votes, and those votes are themselves shaped by the ranking.

That last clause creates a feedback loop with an old mathematical name. Imagine two reviews that are equally good. Each new reader is more likely to see, and therefore vote for, whichever one already has more votes. Model it as a Pólya urn.[33] Review 1 has \(a\) votes and review 2 has \(b\). The next vote goes to review 1 with probability \(a/(a+b)\), and the winner's count goes up by one.

Pólya's urn.

Starting from \((a_0,b_0)\), the share \(a_t/(a_t+b_t)\) converges almost surely to a random limit \(Z\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a_0,b_0)\). Starting from one vote each, \(Z\) is uniform on \([0,1]\).

Read that again with two equally good reviews in mind. The long-run share does settle, but where it settles is a coin flip spread evenly over every possible split. A 90–10 outcome is exactly as likely as a 50–50 one. The final ranking records which review got lucky in the first few votes.

The urn is a cartoon, but the effect is real. In Salganik, Dodds, and Watts's MusicLab experiment, letting participants see what others had downloaded made success both more unequal and less predictable across parallel worlds.[34] In Muchnik, Aral, and Taylor's randomised experiment on a social news site, a single artificial up-vote on a comment raised the chance that the next viewer also rated it positively by 32 percent and lifted its final score by 25 percent on average. Artificial down-votes were largely corrected by later users.[35] On Amazon itself, Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil and coauthors found that whether readers call a review helpful depends not only on its text but on how its star rating compares with the other reviews.[36] "Most helpful" is not a neutral measurement. It is partly a record of earlier ranking decisions.

A careful platform therefore faces a classic dilemma. If it always shows the review with the best current helpfulness ratio, it exploits what it knows, and a new, possibly excellent review with zero votes never gets the exposure it needs to earn any. If it shows new reviews too often, it wastes readers' attention on unproven text. This is the multi-armed bandit problem: each review is an arm with an unknown rate \(\theta_i\) of earning a helpful vote, showing it is a pull, and a vote is the reward.

Thompson's 1933 answer is still the most elegant one I know.[28][32] Keep a Beta posterior for each review, which is exactly the object our shopper used in the Bayes section, and then:

\[ \tilde\theta_i\sim\mathrm{Beta}\bigl(1+h_i,\ 1+n_i-h_i\bigr)\ \text{ for each review } i, \qquad \text{show } \arg\max_i\tilde\theta_i, \]

where \(h_i\) is the number of helpful votes and \(n_i\) the number of times review \(i\) has been shown. After the reader responds, update that review's posterior and repeat.

A review with few votes has a wide posterior, so it sometimes draws a high sample and gets shown. A review with many votes has a narrow posterior and is shown roughly as often as it deserves. Exploration is driven by uncertainty, with no hand-tuned exploration rate. The shopper's belief from earlier in the essay has turned into the platform's ranking policy.

The general version of the platform's problem is a Bellman equation over beliefs. The state \(s\) is the whole collection of posteriors, an action \(a\) is what to show, and

\[ V(s)=\max_a\Bigl\{r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V(s')\Bigr\}. \]

For independent arms with geometric discounting, Gittins proved in 1979 that this enormous problem splits into one index per arm, and the optimal policy is to pull the arm with the highest index.[29] Lai and Robbins showed how fast regret, the shortfall relative to always showing the truly best review, must grow for any policy that eventually learns. For Bernoulli arms with means \(\theta_i\) and best mean \(\theta^\star\),

\[ \liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb E[\mathrm{Regret}_T]}{\log T}\ \ge\ \sum_{i:\,\theta_i\lt\theta^\star}\frac{\theta^\star-\theta_i}{\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\theta_i\,\|\,\theta^\star\bigr)}. \]

Learning has an unavoidable logarithmic price, and arms that look similar, with small KL divergence, are the expensive ones to tell apart.[30] Thompson sampling is known to match this bound for Bernoulli bandits.[32] By 2010, contextual bandits were choosing news stories on Yahoo!'s front page.[31]

One more twist completes the picture. A ranking changes what the platform will learn next, and in the long run it changes what people choose to write. Perdomo and coauthors call predictions that influence the outcomes they predict performative.[40] The data are no longer a fixed sample from the world. Part of the world is now a response to the model.

The whole system

Put the pieces together and the review page stops looking like a list. It looks like a loop of decisions, each with its own beliefs, utilities, and costs.

This is what "decision systems" means to me now. The shopper is an expected-utility maximiser with a threshold, a reading cost, and, being human, some recency. The platform is a sequential decision-maker learning from votes it partly causes. The votes are an urn. The reviews are evidence whose usefulness depends on where they are placed. Each piece is a textbook model on its own. The system is what you get when you connect them and let the loop run.

Now try to break it

This is the part I would make longer if this were a pitch deck.

  1. Reviews are not conditionally independent. If later reviewers read earlier ones, the shopper's likelihood ratios double-count. A Bayesian who multiplies ten correlated signals as if they were independent is overconfident in a perfectly coherent way.
  2. The likelihoods are adversarial. Fake reviews are written to imitate real ones, so \(P(+\mid L)\) is a strategic choice, not a constant. At that point decision theory becomes game theory, which is where my pricing and mechanism design essays live.
  3. The utility table is invented. Real people often do not have stable preferences waiting to be measured. They construct them while choosing. The axioms describe consistency, not where the numbers come from.
  4. Reading cost is not constant. People skim stars, jump straight to the one-star reviews, sort by "most recent," or read only the review with a photo. Each habit is a different stopping rule with a different order effect.
  5. The platform's objective is not the shopper's. A ranking tuned for conversion may differ from one tuned for informed purchases, and returns are where that gap shows up.
  6. My parameters are illustrative. The belief-adjustment strength \(s=0.2\), the likelihoods 0.8 and 0.4, and the reading cost 0.05 were chosen to make the mechanics visible. The sign and size of order effects on a real platform are empirical questions, and that is exactly the kind of question the Amazon paper set out to study.[1]

What this story does not say

It does not say people are irrational because their beliefs depend on order. A costly-attention Bayesian shows order effects too. It does not say expected utility is wrong because Allais and Ellsberg exist. It says the normative and descriptive theories answer different questions. It does not say helpfulness rankings are useless. It says they are decisions with feedback, and they deserve the care we give any other decision.

It also makes no claim about the specific estimates in Kaushik, Mishra, Rana, and Dwivedi's paper. I have used its question and its theoretical ingredients as the door into this subject. For its data, model, and results, go to the paper.[1] All earphone prices, utilities, likelihoods, and costs in this essay are made up for the illustration.

Back to the two tabs

I did not run expected utility on physics versus data science. I'm not that far gone. But looking back with this essay's vocabulary, that second-year semester looks a lot like the review page.

I was sitting near a threshold, where information is worth the most. The choice was expensive to reverse. And there was a cheap experiment available: a summer at IIM Nagpur, working on real text data with someone whose job is literally decision science. In Howard's terms, it was sample information with high EVSI. In Simon's terms, it was a sensible place to search before stopping. It was also, without my planning it, a very good first review. It came early, it was positive, and it pushed me out of the band. A Bayesian with limited attention would recognise the pattern immediately.

The paper that started it was asking whether the order of reviews changes what people buy. The ground-up answer is the best kind of annoying. For a perfect Bayesian reading everything, no. For a person, often yes. For a perfect Bayesian who has better things to do than read every review, also yes, but in the opposite direction. And for the platform choosing the order, that question is not academic at all. It is the decision.

A rational shopper would shuffle the reviews. Nobody shuffles the reviews. That gap is where decision theory gets interesting.
